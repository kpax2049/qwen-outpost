WHY IT READS
Warm green ground sits opposite every machine's cool grey metal, so buildings pop off terrain by temperature, not by outline weight. Amber is reserved for tech light and UI accent — nothing in nature is amber, so anything glowing amber is something you built.
CHARACTER
This is the most classic-adventure of the three: legible daylight, dense foliage, a person on foot. It suits the exploration half of the loop — walking out to find deposits feels like a hike, not a map query.
RISK
Green ground plus green foliage is the narrowest value range of the three, so belts and machines carry most of the contrast. If you want maximum machine legibility, 2b or 2c beats it.

WHY IT READS
The widest value range of the three: near-white snow, near-black basalt. Machines are mid-grey, so they separate from both extremes, and the two accents do exactly one job each — cyan means running, magenta means broken. The mockup runs one generator against 70 ⚡ of demand, so it shows two failures at once: the grid is 20 ⚡ short (magenta HUD chip) and the near smelter is jammed (magenta bar and flag) — everything still working stays calm cyan.
CHARACTER
The strongest mood and the clearest sci-fi read. A drone instead of a person makes the outpost feel remote-operated and slightly lonely — which fits a game about automating yourself out of the loop.
RISK
Cold palettes get monotonous over long sessions, and snow's brightness fights the HUD. Wood as a resource makes least sense on an ice shelf — the frozen pines are a fiction we'd need to keep leaning on.

WHY IT READS
The whole world is warm and the whole machine set is cool grey with cyan light, so built vs. found is a single temperature decision your eye makes before it resolves any shape. It's the cleanest solution to the original problem of green buildings on green ground.
CHARACTER
Salvage-punk with a lot of soul: red canyon, dust, a rover with an antenna. Feels like the outpost was dropped somewhere already picked over — and it gives the terrain the most visual variety of the three.
RISK
Red sandstone competes with iron and copper ore, so ore nodes lean on outline and highlight rather than hue. Long sessions in an all-warm palette can feel dusty; the cyan accent is what keeps it from going sepia.
WHAT'S SHARED, AND WHAT I'D PICK
All three share one deliberate constant: the machine silhouette grammar — derrick, dome, arch, tier, gantry, crate stack. That's the part doing the readability work, and it shouldn't be a stylistic variable. What actually differs between directions is biome, palette temperature, accent logic, and who the player is. The conveyor is carried over from the last round untouched in behaviour: treaded band, static direction wedge, motion only while items advance.

Everything here still respects the source: ITEM_COLORS hues on items and nodes, 48px logical tiles (shown at 64px), and no change to costs, power, recipes or states.
My recommendation: 2c, with 2b's accent discipline. Rust Basin solves the original readability problem most directly — a warm world against cool machines — and has the most world identity per pixel. Borrowing 2b's strict two-accent rule (one colour for running, one for broken) would give it the state clarity that Cryo Station gets for free.
