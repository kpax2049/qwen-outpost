Outpost · direction 2a, refined · round 3

RELAY SEVEN.

2a taken from homage to production. The single biggest change is resolution: every sprite is redrawn at 32×32 native and shown at 2×, so the tile is the same size on screen but carries four times the pixels. That's what buys grounded terrain texture, real mechanical detail on machines, and a player who is a person rather than a shape — without touching the crispness, the framing, the progress language or the conveyor.

01 — Terrain

The old tiles were built from motifs on a flat fill, which is what made them feel gamey. These are built the way a tileset is: a broad tonal mass first, then irregular patches, then detail, then noise. Nothing repeats on a visible rhythm, and dirt is new — it's the surface the outpost actually sits on, so built areas read as ground that has been worked.

02 — Resource nodes

The old nodes were stacks of geometric lumps. These are deposits: each one sits in a disturbed ground scar, so it looks embedded rather than dropped, and each is built on a different physical idea — sawn timber, fractured boulders, ore banded through a rock matrix, glossy coal seam, quartz with gold veining. Ore keeps its source ITEM_COLORS hue but now sits in grey rock, so the hue is the signal and the matrix is the contrast.

Function is drawn, not implied. Every machine now carries the mechanism that does its job: a bit you can see cutting, a drum you can see burning, a mouth you can see pouring, an arm you can see moving, a hatch you can see opening. That's the difference between "grey box with a colour" and a silhouette you can name from across the map.
Hazard striping is a signal, not decoration. Only the four powered process machines wear the amber-and-black skirt — miner, smelter, steel furnace, generator. Storage, chest and the assembler don't, so the passive and clean-process buildings separate from the dangerous ones before you read any detail.

06 — Composed gameplay

Dirt spreads where the outpost has been worked, so the base reads as a site rather than machines dropped on a lawn. Flow is unambiguous: the trunk line runs westward from the miner to the smelter — left-pointing chevrons, left-scrolling treads, ore travelling left — then turns north, carrying ingots up to the assembler on up-pointing chevrons. Power is computed from what's actually placed: 2 generators = 100 ⚡ against miner 5 + smelter 10 + steel 20 + assembler 15 + 11 belts = 61 ⚡, leaving +39.

4 · PROGRESS METER — EMBEDDED INSTRUMENTATION

The soft pill is gone. The meter is now an inset slot cut into the machine's plinth: a black recess with a hard 1px shadow line at top and a lit metal lip along the bottom, so it looks milled into the chassis rather than floated over it. The channel is divided into eight 6.25px cells and the fill advances in eight discrete steps, one cell per step, not a smooth slide — a cycle counter on a machine, which also makes partial progress countable at a glance. It borrows the amber-on-black language already used by the hazard skirts and stack bands, so nothing new enters the palette.
5 · TERRAIN — GRASS CALMED, WATER REBUILT
GRASS
WATER
Grass was reading as canopy because it carried 44 high-contrast blade marks per tile — the same density and value jump the forest tile uses, so at a glance the eye classified it as vegetation-from-above. Blade count is down to seven low-contrast tufts, the tonal patches are softened to a much narrower range, and the worn-dirt flecks are removed entirely. It now reads as flat ground cover that tiles quietly across a large map, and it stops competing with the forest.

Water was a dithered checker that looked like static. It's rebuilt as real water: a five-stop depth gradient down the tile, two darker pools for volume, and three long smooth sine swells with sparse foam caught on their crests. Value is deep enough to sit convincingly beside the dirt and rock, it reads unmistakably as impassable liquid, and there's no cartoon sparkle.
Rationale, in one place
Why 32px fixed the pastiche problem. At 16px every asset has to be a symbol, and symbols read as homage. At 32px the same object can be described — a drill has a bit and a mast, a boiler has bands and a firebox — so it reads as a modern pixel-art game that grew out of 16-bit rules instead of imitating them. Nothing about the on-screen scale changed.

Why terrain got quieter. Ground now uses low-contrast noise and broad tonal masses; the loud contrast is reserved for objects. That's what stops it looking cartoonish while making machines and nodes pop harder than before.
Why dirt earned a tile. The outpost needed a floor. Dirt spreading around built areas gives the base a footprint and a history, and it separates "your site" from "the wild" without any UI overlay. It's also the single cheapest addition to the world's character.

Why nodes sit in scars. A deposit drawn as loose lumps floats; drawn with disturbed ground under it, it belongs to the tile. The scar also gives every node a dark base that separates it from whatever terrain it lands on.
Why hazard striping is rationed. Used everywhere it became texture. Restricted to the four powered process machines, it becomes information — dangerous industry versus passive storage — and it gives the palette its industrial-retro-futurist accent without neon.

What is deliberately unchanged. The conveyor (rails, rollers, chevrons, motion only while items advance), the frame-and-bevel panel language, the amber accent rule, and every number: costs, power, capacities, recipes and the six engine statuses.
Direction check: every belt in this document uses a direction-specific sprite whose chevrons match both its tread scroll and its item travel — east/west/north/south strips in section 07 and both runs in the gameplay composition. Arrow direction and animation direction agree everywhere.
